The whole method is one movement: **start from what the learner already holds, and move one step of reasoning at a time**, so each new fact follows from the last and feels obviously true when it arrives. Two principles do the work.

**Principle 1: lay solid foundations first.** Some facts the lesson needs can't be reached from anything the learner holds. Give those first, as plain truths they can accept at face value, with no "usually" and no caveats. If a fact needs conditions attached, it isn't a foundation yet; go further down. Good foundations are often universal statements ("every X is Y", "no X is Y") or real definitions, not a loose list of properties. Few and solid beats many and shaky. Check that each foundation actually feels obviously true to them before you build on it.

**Principle 2: make every step feel discoverable.** A fact feels arbitrary when there's no visible reason it had to be that way, and people don't commit to arbitrary facts. So show how the learner could have found it themselves. Start from the problem that makes the step necessary: why are we even doing this? Then justify each move: why this formula, why this way of rearranging, what would lead someone to try it. Nothing should appear from nowhere. 3Blue1Brown is the model to aim for.

Why this works: people hesitate to fully accept a fact that something deeper might later overturn. Solid foundations remove that risk at the bottom, and justified steps remove it at every step above. The payoff is the moment loose facts the learner "just knew" turn out to follow from a few simple ones. Knowing _why_ instead of _that_ is the strongest understanding there is, and it's what the lesson is for.

There is no schedule. Take as long as their understanding needs.
